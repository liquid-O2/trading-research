  THE CASE03
 Meet the average unprofitable trader

  Most people watching a case study expect a disaster story: someone reckless, someone
  lazy, someone who never learned the concepts. Keani is not that. He's average, and
  average is the problem.

  Keani, who goes by Alive, has been trading Nasdaq futures since September 2025, and
  his path was better than most. A friend steered him away from ICT and into order flow; he
  joined the Data server, learned AMT from the basics up, moved on to MotiveWave. Daily-
  chart reference points, the 30-minute TPO, poor highs and poor lows, value. On paper,
  that's a framework.

  But he had a job, which meant he had eval money, and that's where the spiral started.
  Eval after eval, each one bought on the feeling that this time he knew enough. He went
  from blowing evals to passing some. And then the sentence that tells you everything:



  "Whenever I did pass the evals, I just felt like I got lucky doing it... that
  I actually didn't deserve it." 01:03


  He passed and still didn't trust it, and he knew he couldn't prove it either way. That
  feeling, "I got lucky and I can't prove otherwise," is the actual subject of this PDF.
  Everything that follows is about replacing it with data.

  THE DIAGNOSIS07
 The red list: four mistakes

  Before I drew a single chart in that call, I wrote four things in red, straight out of Keani's
 own answers in the first five minutes. I'd write the same four for most struggling traders I
  meet.


  01   No journaling, no data collection. His words: "I don't journal at all." 02:51 His entire data operation was
        a daily thesis in a Discord, glanced back at a week later. A thesis on a server is an opinion with a

  02   Assumption. His best winner of the week was a short during a Trump speech: "I just assumed that we

  03   Outdated books. Trading books ten, twenty years old, treated as current. Reading feels like work, but
         trading information is data, and old data ages. Two-decade-stale inputs are competing against inputs

        him, switching approach every time the answer hurt. Nobody abandons a strategy they can prove works.
        Hopping is what the absence of proof feels like from the inside.

  sufficient data to even show that your strategy is profitable." 06:36

  THE DIAGNOSIS09
 The psychology myth

  Beginners believe losing streaks and over-trading are psychology problems. I want to
  push back on that hard, because it's the most expensive belief in this whole case. "It's
  just so easy to blame one, right, which is psychology." 06:23 Psychology is vague, so it
  absorbs blame without ever producing a fix. Ask the obvious question first:



  "You don't data collect. So how do you even know what you're doing is
  correct in the first place?" 05:54


  Without data you cannot distinguish a psychology problem from a strategy problem from
  plain variance. Three losses in a row might be tilt. It might also be a 70% win-rate
  strategy doing exactly what a 70% win-rate strategy does. You cannot know which
  without a sample, and blaming your mind is the easiest way to avoid building one.

  So here's what you build instead. Picture a box. Inside it is everything your discretionary
  framework permits: say absorption, exhaustion, trapped buyers and sellers. Outside it
  sits everything else the market offers: trend lines, support and resistance, the regimes
  that don't suit you. Inside the box you can act. Outside it, you don't trade, full stop. That
  boundary is your discretionary barrier, and defining it is the first act of data collection,
  because you can only measure a strategy that has edges. Once the box exists, noise
  becomes visible: trading daily-VP shelves off the DOM and randomly switching to
  footprint imbalances mid-session is noise by definition. Before the box, it just felt like
   flexibility.

  The honest caveat: real psychological pressure exists, and data doesn't delete it. It tells
  you whether psychology is actually your problem this week. Most weeks, for most
  beginners, it isn't.

  THE DIAGNOSIS11
 Risk that survives you


  "Your risk management should allow you to over-trade." 10:10


  Read that again, because every influencer tells you the opposite: become a robot who
  never takes a revenge trade. That's a fantasy. You're human, you will over-trade, and
  pretending otherwise means your risk plan assumes a person who doesn't exist. Design
  for the person who does.

  From my own data collection, I know it takes me two to three losses before I notice I've
  tipped into over-trading: "my risk management allows me to survive those two to three
  losses." 10:25 So my per-trade risk is sized so that two to three unplanned losses don't
  matter. The mistake gets absorbed by the structure before I've even caught it. That's the
  standard: risk sized for your worst documented behavior, and you only know your worst
  behavior if you've collected it.

 Now compare Keani's answer when I asked how he sizes: "I risk just based off of, like, in
  the moment how I'm feeling and what the account needs." 11:58 And the worse admission
  underneath: when he suspects he's revenge trading, he sizes up. Maximum size at the
 moment of minimum judgment. Emotions cannot be quantified, so they cannot be a
  sizing input, ever.

 On the board, "static $200" got the strikethrough. Beginners risk the same dollar figure
  on an A setup and a C setup, which makes no sense once you say it out loud. Best setups
  deserve more money, worst deserve less: size flexes with setup quality, while targets
  don't flex at all, staying where the auction logically pays. Keani had half the instinct
  already, grading by confluences. What he didn't have was any data proving his grades
  meant anything. That's the next chapter.

  THE BLUEPRINT13
 The confluence fallacy

  Keani risks more on setups with more confluences, and he believes, like almost every
  beginner, that more confluences means a higher chance of winning. Nobody ever checks
   it. So let's check it.

  Take two trades. Trade A has three confluences and you risk $150. Trade B has four and
  you risk double. Both make complete sense technically. Is the extra risk on B justified?
  You'd need the hit rate of each, and Keani has neither number. His evidence was memory
  and old Discord posts. He was doubling his risk on a feeling that the fourth confluence
  matters.



  "You're comparing it to nothing, because you have no data backing
  that hypothesis up." 18:58


 Now the worked example from the board, the single most useful piece of math in this
  PDF. Say you collect 20 trades on three-confluence setups and find a 70% win rate at 1:5
  risk-reward. Now demand a fourth confluence. Plausibly the win rate drops to 65%,
  maybe 60%. How, if the setup got stricter? Because the fourth confluence costs you: time
  hunting for it, fewer trades taken, good setups going untraded. The stricter filter can
  genuinely produce a worse account. Or a better one. You cannot know which until
  you've collected the 20 trades, and Keani, months in, had collected zero. His dashboard
  adds a second cost: his account can't afford lower frequency, so for him specifically,
  stacking probably makes things worse.

  To be fair: sometimes the fourth confluence does raise the hit rate. The fallacy was never
  in adding confluences. It's in adding them unmeasured.

  THE BLUEPRINT15
 The product you actually trade

  I asked Keani if he understands how a prop firm works. "No, I don't think so." Most
  funded traders would have to give the same answer, and it costs them the payout. You
  think you're trading the market. You're not:



  "A prop firm is a synthetic product... You're not actually trading a real-
  life market. You're trading a barrier-based product." 14:04


 A live account sends real orders to the CME. A prop account is a demo with two barriers
  painted on it: a loss limit below you, a payout threshold above you. Touch one and you're
  out, touch the other and you get paid. That's the entire game. And the barriers move: an
  end-of-day trailing drawdown rises with your profit and buffer. The rules are dynamic, so
  risking a static dollar amount inside a product with moving walls is a category error. You
  manage risk to the barriers, because the barriers are the product.

  Once you see it as a product, the economics invert. You pay roughly $150 for an account
  that can pay out $2,000, so your real maximum loss is the fee. Professional profitability is
  alpha, and alpha takes maths, statistics, capital and equipment you don't have. Here you
  don't need it: "You don't need to have a positive expected-value strategy. You only need
  variance. Your variance has to be profitable." 36:20 About three weeks of technical
  profitability, repeated: five accounts, three hit the payout barrier for $2K each, two die for
  $150 each, net positive even if the strategy is marginal. The caveat is a big one: variance
  without process is gambling with extra steps, and none of this guarantees a payout for
  anyone, ever.

  THE AUDIT17
 The four questions

  Before the interview I wrote four questions on the board. They were the skeleton of
  everything I asked Keani, and they're the audit I'd hand any struggling trader. Answer
  them honestly, in writing, before you buy your next eval.


  01   Do you collect data? Not "do you think about your trades." Not "do you post a thesis in a Discord
        sometimes." Do you have, written down, a sample of your own trades tagged by feature, regime and
        outcome, large enough to answer a question with? Keani's honest answer was no, and if yours is too,
        every other problem you think you have is unverified. This question comes first because every later
         question depends on it.

  02   Do you understand how a prop firm works? Can you explain, right now, how your account's trailing
       drawdown moves, where both barriers sit today, and what that implies for your size? If you're risking a
         fixed dollar amount inside a product whose walls move daily, the answer is no, whatever you'd like it to
         be.

  03   What is your main issue? (It's probably not the main issue.) This is the trap question. Keani said
         psychology. Nearly everyone says psychology, because psychology is unfalsifiable and flattering: it
         implies the strategy is fine and only the pilot needs work. But what feels like a discipline problem is
         usually a data problem or a risk problem casting a shadow. You over-trade because nothing in your risk
        design absorbs it. You hesitate because you have no sample proving the setup deserves trust.
         Interrogate the diagnosis before you treat it.

  04   Have you optimised your entries? Deliberately last. Entries are where beginners start, and they're the
         least of it. Optimising an entry inside a strategy with no data, emotional sizing and an unread product is
         polishing the door handles on a house with no foundations. Once the first three questions have real
        answers, entry optimisation becomes a measurable A/B test like everything else.

  THE BLUEPRINT19
 The data-collection system

  "Is journaling the best way to collect data?" Keani asked. The system is smaller and more
  boring than people expect. Two kinds of data: quantitative, the numbers a dashboard
  spits out; and qualitative, the written record of what you saw and why you acted.
  Beginners need the qualitative side first, because it tells you what to count.

   It starts with the daily thesis: where are we in the higher-timeframe auction, and who
  should be in control? Holding a short at the lower boundary of the curve? Possibly not.
  Buyers step in at a discount. "Thesis is extremely important, to filter your trades. As a
   filter." 22:08

  Then entries, and one term worth learning: the feature set. Thesis is a feature, entry is a
  feature; deeper, absorption is one, trapped buyers and sellers another, aggression
  another. Each is testable. Collect 10 to 20 trades per feature and let the sample declare
  the winner: absorption against trapped traders, level one against level two, drop the
  loser. Write the regime down every time, fade or continuation, because features are
  regime-specific: absorption suits fading a trend; trapped sellers pile up at the bottom of
  continuation trends.



 "Why are you asking me for a subjective judgment? You need to
  actually look at the day for yourself." 25:18


  That was my answer when Keani asked whether his p-day and b-day rule was "good," and
  I used to ask the same of better traders. Whatever anyone tells you is their judgment on
  their trades. Collect 20 to 30 of your own; if the rule made no difference, it's noise, drop
   it.

  THE BLUEPRINT21
 Worked example: open above value

  Late in the call, Keani laid out his own setup, and it's worth walking through because it
  shows what a framework sounds like once the vagueness is squeezed out.

  His scenario: the current day's TPO opens fully above yesterday's value, A period sitting
  clear of the prior value area high. He waits until around 10:00 for the day type to
  establish from the shape of the profile, then watches the current day's value area high for
  a break and a retest, entering off the volume profile.

  I drew it out, and we validated it through AMT rather than through "does it look good."
  Sequence matters, so follow it in order. Value opening and building higher is the market
  telling you buyers are in control and sellers are getting exhausted. Price will probably
  reject off the POC or the previous day's value area high. Once we're above it and price
  breaks the current value area high, you should see aggressive buying imbalances doing
  the breaking. Then the test that decides everything: price comes back to those
  imbalances. If the buyers who broke the level defend them, that retest is a good long. If
  they fold, you have your answer, and it cost nothing.

  Notice what made the setup good. Every step is the auction confirming the same story
  from a different angle: value shift, rejection, aggressive break, defended retest. No step
   is decoration.

 Now the caveats, because I gave Keani these on the call and they're load-bearing. The
  trade is still subject to time of day, still subject to what the DOM shows at the retest, and
   it still needs a decent higher-timeframe objective, somewhere for the auction to actually
  go. A clean break-and-retest into nothing is a clean entry into a losing trade. Thesis first,
  always. The entry is the last gate, never the first.

  THE BLUEPRINT23
 How to journal: the recording protocol

  People hear "journal" and picture a notebook full of feelings. Wrong tool, and the reason
   it never sticks. Don't journal on paper. Record your screen for the whole session, then
  scrub back to the moment just before each entry, pause there, and write what you were
  thinking at that exact moment.



  "What was I thinking before I entered the trade? That is the most
  important information you'll have." 29:33


  The reason you pause before the entry is lookahead bias. Journal after the trade and the
  known result poisons the record: "A lot of people think if you win, it's a good trade, when
  most of the time it's probably a bad trade. Or if you lose, it's a bad trade, but it might be a
  good trade. It's just variance at that point." 29:51 Keani had been doing exactly this,
  screenshotting after the close. Every entry in that journal is graded by an outcome
  variance already decided.

  THE BLUEPRINT24
 What collection produces

  Write it plainly. "Absorption." "Targeting value area high." Why you entered, what
  happened, how many sellers lost, the volume of the candle. Screenshots in. Money result
  last; by then it barely matters, because the process is what you're grading. What does
  collection buy? Hard numbers like this one from my DOM work: absorption fails 27% of
  the time unless there's a 3-tick aggression toward the absorbed side. A tradeable filter
  that only exists because the trades behind it were counted.

  Two closing points. Tools don't matter: Notion, a journaling site, Discord; collect properly

  THE BLUEPRINT25
 The DOM: Location, Pacing, Digits

  Beginners come to the DOM looking for a magic entry trigger. There isn't one, and hunting
  for it is why most people who "trade the DOM" lose. The DOM is three reads, in a fixed
  order, and the first one isn't even on the DOM.

  One: Location. Before you read a single order, know where you are in the auction,
  because location tells you who should be in control. At the lower boundary of the volume
  profile, price is at a discount and buyers should step in; price seeks the POC and value 60
  to 80% of the time. So you give buyers leniency on the DOM and hold sellers to a higher
  standard: deciding who gets the benefit of the doubt before the tape starts arguing.



   "If you don't understand where we are in the auction, you'll never be
  truly profitable on the DOM, on the footprint, on any other order-flow
  entry trigger." 38:35

  THE BLUEPRINT26
 Pacing, then digits

  Two: Pacing. Effort against reward. A huge divergence of buyers over sellers while price
  stagnates, rotating in the same few ticks, is massive effort with no reward, and effort that
   isn't being paid is a warning, whatever the imbalance says.

  Three: Digits. At the open you can't compute bid-ask maths fast enough, so read digit
  size. Sellers refreshing from two and three-digit sizes down to singles are thinning:
  weakness. Buyers stepping from two and three digits to four and five are building effort.
 When the pacing then upticks, effort finally getting its reward, that's the entry.

  Location, then pacing, then digits, always in that order, always after the thesis. The
  reward systems underneath are in the DOM Advanced video. One warning: only your own
 DOM recordings of your own entries are worth studying. Other people's tape teaches
  other people's trades.





  The DOM in three reads: Location (who should be in control here), Pacing (is effort being rewarded, or is price just rotating,
  the wave at right), Digits (who is thinning to singles, who is building). In that order, always after the thesis.
