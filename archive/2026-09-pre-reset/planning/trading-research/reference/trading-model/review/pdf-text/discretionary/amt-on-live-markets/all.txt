03 What AMT actually is



  the market constantly looking for a price both sides agree on


  At its core, AMT is the idea that the market is constantly trying to find a price where buyers and

  sellers are both willing to do business. Everything else in the framework sits underneath that

  one sentence.


  Price is always in one of two conditions. In balance, both sides are relatively comfortable at

  current prices, which looks like consolidation, chop, price going nowhere with real conviction.

  Imbalanced looks like a clean trend, because one side has gotten more aggressive, willing to

  accept worse and worse prices to keep pushing. Once price moves away from an area both

  sides had agreed on, participants start looking for the next area of agreement, which is usually a

  previous one: somewhere price has already done this before.

04 Fair value and the value area



  not one price, a whole range, and where VAH and VAL come from


  Fair value is the area where the market is comfortable doing business, where you see real,

  significant two sided trade. The correction worth sitting with: fair value is not one exact price

  point. It's a range.


  On a volume profile, that range is the value area, the section of the bell curve containing the

  majority of a session's traded volume. The top of that range is value area high, the bottom is

  value area low, and the space between them, where price spends most of its time chopping, is

  what the rest of AMT is built on top of.



  THE BELL CURVE: VALUE AREA IN THE MIDDLE, THE TAPER AT EACH END

05 Extremes



  the outer edges of the auction, and the one question they force


  Extremes are the same idea as value area, from the other direction: the outer edges of the

  auction, the taper in volume at each end of the bell curve. When price reaches an extreme, there

   is exactly one question worth asking: does the market want to keep accepting prices out here,

  or does it want to reject them and return to fair value.


  That question is more useful than it sounds, because it replaces a guess with an observation.

  You are not predicting what price will do at the extreme. You are watching what it actually does

  once it gets there, and reading that.

06 Acceptance and rejection



  the actual lens AMT gives you, not "buy or sell here"


  This is the most important pair of concepts in AMT, and the reframe is worth stating exactly the

  way he puts it: instead of thinking "price touched this level, so should I buy or sell," think "what

  did the auction do after reaching this level. Did we accept into the range, or did we reject."


  Acceptance means the market is comfortable doing business at those prices. It looks like price

  spending time there and building structure, chopping around a level rather than fleeing it.

  Rejection is the opposite: price enters an area and quickly moves away from it. If fair value was

  established lower and price rejects an upper extreme, the read is straightforward: sellers failed

  to drive price lower there, buyers absorbed them, and price rejected back up. The level told you

  what happened. You didn't have to guess.

07 High volume nodes and low volume nodes



  where price stalls, and where it has no reason to


  Inside a volume profile you'll often see more than one bell curve stacked together, what he calls

  a double distribution. A high volume node is exactly what it sounds like, an area where a lot of

  volume traded, usually because price spent real time there. A low volume node is the opposite,

  a stretch with relatively little trading, typically left behind by a fast move up or down.


  Low volume nodes represent a lack of acceptance, and they tend to exist between two areas of

  established value. When price enters one, the same acceptance-or-rejection question applies:

  does price reject immediately on contact, or does it just keep going, dumping straight through

  because there was never much conviction there to begin with.

08 The failed auction



  drawn from scratch: what failing to find fair value actually looks like


  Put the balance-imbalance-balance cycle and the acceptance question together and you get the

  failed auction. Price is at fair value, sellers gain control, and price gets driven lower, back toward

  a previous area of fair value. If price reaches that lower area and rejects it, coming all the way

  back up rather than accepting into it, that's a failed auction: the market failed to find fair value

  at the lower prices it just tried.


  Once price comes back into the original range, the same acceptance question is waiting again,

  one level up. That's the setup for the odds discussed next.



  THE FAILED AUCTION, DRAWN STEP BY STEP: DRIVEN LOWER, REJECTED, BACK INTO RANGE

09 The 80/20 odds, and how POC tells you which
 one



  repeated failure at POC is the tell, not a guess


  Once price is back inside the previous range after a failed auction, he assigns rough odds to

  what happens next: 80 percent that price runs all the way to the far extreme, 20 percent that it

  simply ranges inside instead. The number itself matters less than the tool he uses to tell which

  one you're actually in before it resolves.


  Inside that balance there is a POC. If price keeps coming in and constantly fails to break

  through it and hold, that's a clear sign you're most likely getting the 20 percent case, a session

  that just chops inside fair value. But if buyers push aggressively through POC, maybe with a

  retest that holds, that's the tell you're more likely seeing the 80 percent case play out.

10 Live example: rejection, a retest, and the trip
 back to previous balance



  a deep dip that still failed to hold


  On the chart, price had been going from one extreme to the other inside a defined balance.

  Once it finally rejected and continued lower, a small retest confirmed the move, and the target

  became the previous balance further down, an area where price had already agreed on value

  once before.


  What makes this example worth including rather than a cleaner one: price didn't just tag that

  lower level and bounce. It dipped meaningfully into the previous balance, deep enough that a

  less patient read might have called it accepted. It still failed to actually hold and trade lower

  from inside it. Buyers slowly gained momentum and eventually pushed price all the way back up

  into the newer, higher balance. The rejection was real, it just took longer to confirm than the

  textbook version does.

11 Live example: a week long balance, read on the
 higher timeframe



  extreme to extreme, then a clean break away from it


  The second example zooms all the way out. Price had auctioned inside one balance for around a

  week, moving from extreme to extreme to extreme, repeatedly accepting the same range as fair

  value each time it returned to it.


  Once price finally broke out, a new fair value formed above the old range, and price traded

  inside it only briefly before showing it no longer wanted to participate there either. It broke out

  again, got a small retest, and came all the way back down to the original fair value, this time not

  stopping to trade inside it. Instead of building structure there the way it had for a week, price

  simply dumped straight through and is now holding at the lower extreme instead, the low

  volume node behavior from earlier in the document, on the highest timeframe in this whole

  document.

12 The one question underneath all of it



  where is the market finding acceptance, and where is it rejecting price


  Every concept in this document, balance, fair value, extremes, high and low volume nodes, the

  failed auction, the 80/20 read, exists to answer a single question: where is the market finding

  acceptance, and where is it rejecting price. That's the actual foundation of auction market

  theory, not a specific setup.


  Once that question becomes the lens you look through by default, the setups in this document

  stop being separate tricks to memorize and start looking like the same read, applied at

  whatever level price happens to be testing right now.

13 The checklist



  every rule in this document, printable


  READING BALANCE AND VALUE


        I know whether price is currently in balance or imbalanced.


      I'm treating fair value as a range (VAH to VAL), not a single price.



  AT THE EXTREME


      I'm asking "did the auction accept or reject here," not "should I buy or sell here."


     A deep dip into a level isn't automatically acceptance. I've confirmed it actually held and traded there.



  HIGH AND LOW VOLUME NODES


        I know whether the level in play is a high volume node (expect a stall) or a low volume node (expect a slice
       through).



  THE 80/20 READ


      I'm checking POC behavior rather than defaulting to the 80% case.


        I can point to repeated failure to hold above or below POC before assuming who's in control.
