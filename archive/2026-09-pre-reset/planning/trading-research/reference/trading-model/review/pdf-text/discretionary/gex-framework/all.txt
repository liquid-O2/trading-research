What an option actually is


An option is a contract. It gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a set price (the strike)
by a set date (the expiration). That one idea, the right without the obligation, is what makes the whole market behave
the way it does.



   THE APPLE FARMER

    It is winter. Apples are coming in summer, so the harvest equipment will be in higher demand and cost more, say 300
   now and 800 later. So you pay the seller 100 today, a premium, to reserve today's price for the summer. If it is worth
   more later, you buy at the reserved price and you are up. If not, you walk away. Your only loss is the premium, and the
    seller keeps it either way. That is an option.


One language note that trips up orderflow traders: in options, a long is a call and a short is a put. Same idea as
futures, just wrapped in a contract. Every one of those contracts has to be hedged by a dealer, and that hedging is
what reaches across into NQ and ES.



   CALL = LONG                                    PUT = SHORT

   The right to buy at the strike. You profit if price rises.          The right to sell at the strike. You profit if price falls.

What really moves NQ and ES


The real engine behind ES and NQ is options flow. Options flow is the stream of options trades that creates hedging
pressure in the futures market, pressure dealers must respond to in real time. Most of that flow feeds straight back
into the futures you trade.

NQ and ES are derivatives.

Derivative comes from the word derive: they come from something else. Think of universities. There is no single
university, but they all teach from the same curriculum handed down by the board. Price works the same way. The
QQQ options complex is the origin point that NQ derives from, and SPY is the origin for ES. So when you read QQQ
options, you are reading the source that moves NQ.

 QQQ               NQ                ES
   the options origin                                   derives from QQQ                                   derives from SPY


This is why reading the futures chart alone leaves you blind to half the picture. The order that moved your candle
often started in the options market.

Dealer hedging, in plain English


Market makers are the dealers. They move the market with billions, and they do not want directional risk. They want
to stay delta-neutral. So they take the opposite side of almost every options trade and hedge it instantly.


  A trader buys a call                                                                      dealer sells a call

  A trader buys a put                                                                       dealer sells a put

  A trader sells a call                                                                      dealer buys a call

  A trader sells a put                                                                       dealer buys a put


So dealers inherit the opposite risk of the trader, but they do not want it. They hedge it away instantly because they
have so much capital to protect. This hedging is mechanical, automatic and constant, run by algorithms, not human
discretion.


    Dealer hedging is a force, and it is not optional. It leaves a footprint in the futures every single session. The rest of GEX
     is just measuring how big that force is and which way it pushes.

Long gamma vs short gamma


GEX measures the dollar value of futures dealers must buy or sell to stay delta-neutral for a given move in price. The
sign of that number is everything.


  LONG GAMMA                             SHORT GAMMA
    Positive GEX, stabilising                                        Negative GEX, amplifying

        Volatility gets suppressed                                                Volatility expands

       Price pins near big strikes                                     Breakouts run, levels break

     Ranges and mean reversion                                   Trends and momentum
     RANGE      TREND

   When dealers are short gamma, a move forces them to trade in the same direction as price: they buy higher and sell
    lower. That feedback loop, lower prices triggering more dealer selling, is why the sharpest trend days happen in
    negative gamma.

The gamma flip


One price matters more than any other: the level where net dealer gamma crosses from positive to negative. It is
called the zero gamma level, or the gamma flip, and it is the most useful line on the whole GEX map.





Net GEX by strike. Red bars are negative gamma (amplifying), green bars are positive (stabilising). The gamma flip is
where they cross; the call wall and max pain are marked too.

Above the flip, dealers are net long gamma, so the regime is stabilising: expect range and mean reversion. Below the
flip, dealers are net short gamma, so it is amplifying: expect trends and expansion. Crossing the flip intraday is a
regime change, and one of the highest-value events you can watch for.

Why this matters for you


Once you see hedging as a force, price behaviour that looked random starts to make sense. Dealer gamma is why:

       Price reverses with no news
       You have all seen price rip into a level and reverse with zero news behind it. That is not random, it is dealer
        hedging defending a strike. Once you know the regime you stop getting trapped by these.


       Breakouts fail at certain strikes
         In long gamma dealers fade strength, so a breakout into heavy positioning gets absorbed and snapped
         back. If you are buying that breakout blind, you are walking straight into their hedging.


       Trend days happen below the gamma flip
       Below the flip dealers are short gamma and have to chase, buying higher and selling lower. That is the fuel
        behind the big one-way days where every pullback just gets bought.


      Range days happen above the gamma flip
       Above the flip dealers are long gamma and suppress every move, so price chops and mean reverts. Trying to
        run breakouts up here just bleeds the account.


      0DTE can control the whole session
       0DTE is now a huge chunk of the flow, and because it expires today it carries the entire session. That is
         exactly why you read QQQ at 0DTE, not a contract expiring next month.



     It always comes back to this: how much hedging pressure are dealers under, and in which direction. Answer that and
   you know the kind of day to expect, more reliably than staring at the VIX.

Regimes, the real mistake


A market regime is a persistent structural environment in which price behaves. It is not just volatility being high or
low. It is the statistical character of orderflow, liquidity and participation over a stretch of time. You have probably
trained your eye to one regime without realising it.

Most traders build a strategy that works in ONE regime,
then trade it in ALL of them.

When the regime shifts, they say my edge stopped working. It did not. The environment changed. The fix is not a new
strategy, it is knowing which regime you are in and only running the layer of your edge built for it. That is why a
serious strategy has layers: setups for trend days, and different setups for chop.



   TREND LAYER                                    CHOP LAYER

   high R, continuation, breakouts                                  scalps, failed auctions, fades

GEX as a regime filter


GEX is shorthand for how the market is positioned in gamma and what that implies for hedging. Read as a regime, it
tells you the day type before the open.


  SHORT / NEG GAMMA                      LONG / POS GAMMA
   Trend day                                             Chop day

      Higher R, target the expansion                                  Smaller R, scalp rotations

      Continuation and breakouts                                       Failed auctions and fades

     Fewer trades, let it run                                         Skip the breakout chases
     TREND       CHOP

   GEX does not tell you exactly where to enter. It tells you how the day will move, so you pick the right kind of setup and
    target. Context first, then confirmation, then execution.

What GEX answers


GEX is not a signal, it is a behavior filter. It will never tell you to buy or sell. It tells you how the day is wired to move,
so you stop forcing the wrong style onto the wrong session.



   QUESTION ONE                                  QUESTION TWO

    Is hedging dampening moves or amplifying them?           Where are the strike walls that matter today? The
   Long gamma dampens and stabilises, short gamma              levels where hedging clusters, the call wall, the put
   amplifies and destabilises. This is the single most               wall and the gamma flip, are the lines price actually
   important read of the day.                                     reacts to.



   Use GEX as a behavior filter. In stabilising regimes expect more pinning and mean reversion. In destabilising regimes
   expect more trend and fast traverses straight through levels.



    Before the open, answer both questions for the session: are dealers calming the tape down or speeding it up, and
   which strikes are they defending. That is your entire game plan in two lines, and it decides every setup you take that
    day.

Pinning and gamma squeezes


Two behaviors fall straight out of the regime, and naming them is half the battle. One quietly kills trends, the other
creates the violent ones.



   PINNING — STRIKE GRAVITY                      GAMMA SQUEEZE — CONVEXITY

   Spot spends time near a strike into expiration. Near         A move where dealer hedging reinforces direction.
   the money gamma is highest, so when hedging is                Price pushes through a major strike, hedging demand
    stabilising, price gets pulled into rotations around the           increases with the move, and it accelerates on thin
    strike. The auction has a local attractor because                   liquidity and hedge chasing. It can happen both up and
   hedging dampens every breakout attempt.                  down.



   Many sessions are simply range days because hedging pressure punishes extension. When the dominant flow is selling
    into upticks and buying into downticks, realised volatility compresses and the market rotates instead of trending.



    Pinning looks like rotations that keep failing at a strike. A squeeze looks like that same strike breaking on aggression
   and then accelerating away. Same level, opposite behavior, and the regime is what tells you which one to expect.

The key levels


Within a regime, a few strikes do most of the work. Treat them as context, never as guarantees.





The GEX levels on price: call walls above (resistance), put walls below (support), the gamma flip in the middle, and max
pain at the bottom.

CALL WALL        Largest call gamma above. Resistance as dealers sell into it.


PUT WALL         Largest put gamma below. Support as dealers buy into it.


MAX PAIN          Strike where option value is lowest. Price often drifts toward it into expiry.



    In long gamma the walls are hard boundaries, so fading toward them works. In short gamma the same walls break
    easily, so trade the breakout through them.

Where gamma lives: strikes


Gamma is not spread evenly. It clusters at strikes, and the closer a strike is to price, the more sensitive it is. That is
where hedging pressure concentrates.



   ATM                             OTM                             ITM

    Strike near price. Highest                Cheaper. Can create large              Behaves more like the stock.
   gamma, most reactive to            gamma shifts if price                  Lower gamma, more delta-heavy.
   movement.                            approaches.





Gamma stacked by strike. The biggest concentrations are where hedging pressure clusters, and where price tends to react.


  Gamma concentrates near the money, and hedging intensity rises as time-to-expiry compresses. That is why 0DTE
   near the big strikes is where the strongest pinning and squeezes happen.

Vanna and charm


Gamma is the headline, but two slower forces explain the drift you see with no news. You do not need to calculate
them, just know they exist and which way they push.



   VANNA

    Delta's sensitivity to implied volatility. When IV falls, dealers must buy to re-hedge. That is the engine behind the steady
   grind higher after a VIX drop, the vol-compression rally.




   CHARM

    Delta's decay as time passes. Into expiry it quietly pulls price toward the biggest open-interest strikes. It is a big part of
   why quiet days melt up toward max pain and the call wall.


Put it together: a calm tape with IV drifting down into a large expiration gives dealers two reasons to keep buying.
That is why low-volatility regimes grind higher and drift toward the high open-interest strikes.

Confirming with orderflow


This is the part most options-flow content skips, and it is the whole edge. GEX gives you context. Orderflow gives you
the trigger. Never trade a GEX level on assumption alone.

       In long gamma, confirm the fade
         Price drifts up into the call wall or an extreme. Before you fade it, watch the DOM and the footprint for
         absorption and real selling aggression. The dealer flow is on your side here, but the tape has to confirm
       someone is actually defending that level.


       In short gamma, confirm the break
         Levels break easily in negative gamma, so do not sit there fading them. Wait for aggression and refills
        pushing through the level, then trade the continuation. Fading a trend day is how you give it all back.


      Use GEX to size and target
         Short gamma earns the higher R and the wider targets because dealers are amplifying the move. Long
      gamma is smaller R and faster exits. Same setup, the regime tells you how hard to press and how far to
         actually target.



    Context first (the regime), confirmation second (orderflow at the level), execution last. Run it in that order every session
   and you stop guessing.

Your session checklist


      Read the regime first
        Before you look at a single setup, pull QQQ 0DTE and read the gamma regime and where the flip sits. That
       one read frames the whole session.


      Above or below the flip
       Above the flip is range and mean reversion. Below it is trend and expansion. If you remember one line from
          this guide, make it this one.


       Short gamma, trade the trend
        Higher R, continuation and breakout setups, target the expansion and take fewer trades. On a day like this
        you can take one trade and ride the entire move.


      Long gamma, trade the chop
        Smaller R, fade the extremes back toward the walls, scalp the rotations and skip the breakout chases. This
           is where the scalp layer of the model earns its keep.


       Confirm with orderflow
        Never act on a GEX level on its own. Confirm with the DOM and the footprint first. GEX is the context, the
        tape is the trigger.


      Re-check after big moves
       A large impulse can flip the regime mid-session. After a big expansion the read often turns long gamma, so
        update before the next trade.

Common mistakes


The traders who lose money with GEX almost always make one of these. Avoid them and you are ahead of most of
the crowd moving into options flow.

       Trading GEX levels blindly
         This is the big one. So many orderflow traders move into options flow and start trading the pinning and
          call/put levels like they are gospel. They are not, they are heavily assumption based. Use them as context,
        then confirm with the DOM and the footprint.


       Ignoring the regime
       Run your trend model on a long gamma chop day, or scalp fades on a short gamma trend day, and even a
        good setup bleeds you. The regime decides which layer of your edge is even allowed to trade.


       Treating pinning as a guarantee
       A pin is a tendency, not a promise. Time of day matters, the open is not the midday is not the close, and one
        event can break the whole thing. Never size like a pin is certain.


      Not updating after an impulse
      Gamma is not static intraday. After a big expansion the read can flip from short to long gamma, so re-read it
         before the next trade or you are trading the old environment.


       Using long-dated options
       You are an intraday trader, so read 0DTE. Contracts expiring in weeks or months are not what is controlling
         today's session, and reading them just gives you the wrong picture.



    Every one of these is the same error: treating GEX as a signal instead of context. Keep it as context, confirm with the
    tape, and you sidestep all five.
