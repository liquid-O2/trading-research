04 His win rate, before and after



  30 to 40 percent, and the honest gap underneath it


  Before the mentorship his actual win rate sat around 30 to 40 percent. He's careful about the

  next number, calling it a guess rather than something he measured: if he'd traded his own

  the difference between having a model and actually having the discipline to sit inside it.





  His own summary, written mid call: bad reward to risk on his setups, a 30 to 40 percent win rate, no
   discipline, no conviction. What he says the mentorship actually solved sits right underneath it in green, a
  mentor reviewing trades and thesis building, not a new indicator.

06 One more source of levels



 why a KG1 level mattered when nothing else was nearby


  Before, his levels came from one place: high volume nodes he'd drawn himself on the higher

  timeframe, wherever they'd already produced a clean reaction. That works, until price sits

  nowhere near one of them. On a day like that he used to have nothing to trade off, or worse, he'd

  trade the middle of the range anyway.


  The KG1 level gave him a second, statistically backed source to work from, gamma levels

  aligned with his own areas of interest. Not a replacement for his own reading, another input into

  the same thesis. More levels with real data behind them meant more high conviction setups

  instead of forcing a trade because a session felt empty.

07 Trade 1: the red rejection area



  a level, a minor high volume node, a stop above the high


  Both trades on this call came from the same pre session work: levels marked out from his own

  thesis building and profile framing, before a single order went out. The first was a short off a

  level he'd already marked as a key area of resistance, price had rejected from it before. He lined

   it up against a minor high volume node sitting close by, two independent reasons pointing at the

  same price rather than one.


  Stop went above the high of the rejection, target set at 1.5R. Nothing exotic in the mechanics,

  the discipline is entirely in the level selection: he took this because two things agreed, not

  because price simply arrived somewhere round.





  The short marked against the resistance he'd already flagged. Risk above the high in red, the planned
  reward below it in green.

08 Trade 2: the expansion leg



  a second tap, absorption, and a thesis that outran its own target


  Going into the session he'd also written down a second possibility: if price retraced, there was

  room for a bigger move higher, a backtest of the same structure from the other side. When price

  came back down to that level and he saw absorption there, buyers stepping in and holding, he

  took the long. Same 1.5R target as the first trade, same process, same discipline of only acting

  once the level actually printed the reaction he'd written down beforehand.


  What happened next wasn't part of the plan. The move expanded well past the 1.5R target on

   its own, an outcome he credits to the setup being right, not to predicting the size of the leg in

  advance. The target was 1.5R. The market gave him considerably more, which is a different

  thing from having called it.





  The long, entered on absorption at the level. The green zone marks how far the actual move ran past the
   original plan.

09 The risk model neither mentor taught



  trailing convexity, and why a normal breakeven quietly ruins your EV


  Asked what he'd never seen anywhere else, he doesn't point at a setup, he points at the risk

  model: trailing convexity as a way to move a trade to breakeven. His own words on it: it was the

   first time he'd ever seen anyone actually use it that way.

      "If you go normally to breakeven, after a hundred trades, if you
     actually look at the stats it ruins your EV in a problem environment.
    But the way you cater it, it's a much better way to go breakeven."


  The mechanism itself is taught live inside the mentorship rather than summarised here, this

  document is his account of what it changed for him, not a replacement for learning it properly.

  What matters for this case study is the honest framing he uses around it: most traders move to

  breakeven on instinct, assume it's automatically the safe choice, and never check what it does

  to their expectancy over a real sample. He hadn't either, until this was shown to him.

10 Two mentorships, and what neither gave
 him



  order flow from one, strategy from another, habits from neither


  He'd been in two prior mentorships before this one. The first taught him most of his order flow

  basics, reading confirmations, absorption, CVD divergence. The second taught him a scalping

  strategy. Both are, in his own words, real skills he still uses. Neither one taught him what to do

  with a losing trade in the moment it stopped looking clean.


  His description of trading before this mentorship: he'd see a valid order flow signal, take it, and

  then panic the instant it didn't play out immediately, moving his stop, moving his target, second

  guessing a read he genuinely had right. He knew the confluences. He didn't have the conviction

  to sit inside a trade once it stopped feeling comfortable, because nothing was backing that

  conviction with data.




     HIS OWN FRAMING

      Beginner order flow traders lose to what looks like a psychology problem, when it's actually a

       risk management problem. A framework that tells you exactly where to trail, where to take

       partials, where to go breakeven removes the decision a panicking trader makes badly in the

     moment. It isn't a mindset fix, it's a rule that makes the mindset irrelevant.

11 Trading as a business



  the analogy that made data collection non negotiable


  The line that reframed everything for him is the same one used throughout the mentorship:

  trading is a business, and every trade is capital you're putting into it. If you were handing money

  to an actual business and it couldn't show you its expected value, its return on capital, any stats

  at all, you wouldn't invest. An index fund can tell you its average return is 8 to 10 percent with a

  roughly one in fifteen year down year. A trader putting real money into evals, into his own

  account, into his own trading, usually can't tell you any of that about himself.

     "Before you put any money in to invest in yourself into trading, into
    buying problems, into doing that stuff, you need to have data backing it.
    Otherwise there's no point."


  He says this is what changed his own relationship to a losing trade. Once he had a real sample

  behind his levels, a loss stopped being a reason to panic and became a data point his own stats

  already expected. That's the shift he keeps coming back to on the call: not more confidence

  from hoping harder, confidence built on a number he can actually point to.

12 Building a thesis live, on the call



  current auction, price in the AMT cycle, one objective


  Asked to just build a thesis on whatever price was doing at that moment, in real time, this is the

  order he actually works in. First, what the current auction is doing: an imbalance had just

  printed a large move up, and price was starting to find a new area of balance. Second, whether

  price has reacted at this level before, looking left on the chart, which tells him whether the area

   is a real key level or a fresh one nobody's tested yet.


  From there the objective: trading back toward balance, targeting a higher timeframe high

  volume node, the point of control on the yearly chart in this case. Because the move up had

  been large, he also expected a retrace or two before that target, and said so before price had

  done it, which is the actual test of a thesis, stating the expectation first rather than narrating it

  afterward.





  The area he marks red live on the call: a level with a prior reaction behind it, not a line drawn because
   price happened to be nearby.

13 The short he wouldn't take anymore



  a level marked, sellers already absorbed, and the old trade anyway


  He walks through a real example of the kind of short he used to take, back when a valid looking

  order flow confirmation was enough on its own. Reacting purely off confirmations without the

  higher timeframe context meant taking trades against a direction that was already telling on

   itself. Nowadays, seeing a key demand level get tested and sellers absorbed there tells him the

  higher timeframe bias, and a short like this one doesn't get taken at all, regardless of how clean

  the local order flow looks.





  The pattern he used to trade: repeated short entries fought against a level that kept holding. Same setup,
  three attempts, the higher timeframe never agreed with any of them. Zoomed out, that same resistance
  area had sat untouched for two weeks before this leg, and once it finally broke, everything below it
  stopped being a place to sell from, a read the local chart alone never gave him.

14 What he'd tell someone joining tomorrow



  trade the levels, not the middle of nowhere


  Asked directly what he'd focus on in his first month if he joined again tomorrow, his answer

  wasn't a concept, it was a habit: trading only off marked levels, not in the middle of the range

  because a chart looks interesting there. Profile framing itself he considers learnable by most

  people who put the reps in. Actually waiting for a marked level to get hit, doing nothing until it

  does, was the part he personally struggled with.




     HIS OWN CHECKLIST, AS HE DESCRIBED USING IT NOW

     What the market is doing right now. What the market wants to do. Where his levels actually are.

     Where he should trade off them. Nothing on that list is new information, he says, it's the same

      four questions asked in the same order, every single session, until asking them stopped being

      optional.

15 The numbers now



  roughly seven times more efficient, by his own account


  The exact figure gets a little rounded on the call itself, he corrects himself mid sentence

  between 1.3 and 1.6 thousand dollars, so treat it as a range rather than a precise number.

  What's consistent across both figures he gives is the shape of the change: from roughly 7,000

  1,600 dollars spent to make around 11,000 in a recent month since. Same rough profit, a

  fraction of the capital it used to cost him to get there.


  His own description of what actually changed matches everything earlier in this document: "it's

   just been more of a disciplined shift and being able to actually follow my habits." Not a new

  strategy replacing an old one. The same read, executed by someone who finally trusted it

  enough to stop interfering with it mid trade.
