What this paper shows

We measured one idea on 199 million ticks of Nasdaq futures data: price levels built by
large aggressive orders, and the memory of whether those levels were defended when
price returned. Three results follow.



   01 / THE RAW CONCEPT LOSES

   Fading every touch of every level loses −0.285R per trade after costs. Only 42% of touches hold. Anyone
    selling "just fade the level" is selling this line.



   02 / SELECTION FLIPS THE SIGN

  A small statistical model reads each touch's context: has this level held before, was it built by real size,
   where does it sit in the day, and it separates the touches that hold from the ones that break (AUC 0.63
   against a 0.51 placebo). Taking only the best-graded touches flips expectancy to +0.14R per trade, on
   data the model never saw.



   03 / THE EDGE IS THE REFILL

   The profit concentrates almost entirely in one execution style: resting passively inside the level, where
   the defenders reload. The same signals traded with market orders lose money.




We then attack our own result with placebos, reversed splits,
rotating folds and a 100-configuration audit, and we publish the
losing quarter along with the winning ones.

  ETHOS ORDER FLOW / RESEARCH                                                  INTRODUCTION
01Every trader has seen it

  Price falls into a level. Heavy selling hits the bid. Nothing happens. The offers absorb, the
  level refuses to break, and price leaves. When price comes back to that level later, it
  holds again.

  Traders call the mechanism a refill: the passive orders defending the level are pulled,
   filled, and quietly replaced. The level "remembers" who defended it, because those
  participants are still there.

  The claim is easy to say and easy to sell. It is much harder to prove. Harvey and Liu
  [2014] showed that most published trading strategies are false discoveries once you
  account for how many things were tried. That warning applies to us as much as to
  anyone.

  So this paper does something unusual for a trading education group: it puts the concept
  through the same machinery an academic would use, and publishes the results either
  way. We audited every input for hindsight, tested against scrambled-label placebos,
  reversed the data split, rotated the folds, and re-ran everything on an independent
  backtest engine we do not control.



 What we found, in one sentence: the refill concept survives, but
  not where most people look for it.


  The entry signal alone is modest. The selection layer (which levels have memory) and the
  execution layer (being the refill, rather than chasing it) carry nearly all of the money.

 We also report what did not survive: raw aggression with no context, tight scalp brackets
  that lived on fill assumptions, and a losing final quarter. All of it is in here, because that is
  what makes the rest believable.

02The refill concept

 When a burst of large aggressive market orders trades at one price area (sixty, eighty, a
  hundred contracts hitting in seconds) that area stops being a random price. Somebody
  with size chose to fight there. We call the cluster of prints a zone.

  The next time price returns to the zone, exactly one question matters: are the defenders
   still there? If they are, the touch is absorbed, the zone refills, and the level holds. If they
  are gone, the zone is just ink on a chart, and price trades straight through it.


 The two outcomes of a touch





  A schematic, not a trade example. A zone is built by clustered large aggressive prints (circles, sized by print size). Price
   leaves, then returns: that is the touch. Everything in this paper exists to answer, beforethetouchresolves, which of these
  two pictures you are in.

 A textbook example, on the real chart





  A real MNQ session in Quant Charts, the platform every statistic here was validated on. Red sell-bubbles hammer a level,
  green buy-bubbles absorb them, and price turns and trends up. That absorption is the refill.

Testing it honestly means turning the idea into numbers. Every zone becomes a row of
data. Every return of price to a zone, every touch, becomes an event with a known
outcome: the level held, or it broke. And every fact we could have known beforethat
outcome becomes a feature: a checklist item a trader could read off the chart in real
time.

That checklist, the feature set, is what this paper is really about. Not an indicator, not a
magic entry: a set of observable facts about a level that, together, tell you whether the
defenders are likely still there.

The next sections measure it end to end: the data and the baseline, where the profit
actually lives, the out-of-sample record, the overfitting battery, and what it means inside
a funded-account evaluation.

Selection is what changes the sign

A deliberately simple statistical model reads the feature checklist for each touch and
produces one number: how likely is this level to hold? It trains only on the past and
grades only on the future, never the other way around. Out-of-sample, the touches it
ranks highest hold 63% of the time; the ones it ranks lowest hold 25%.

Trading everything loses; selecting on memory wins





Left: the average result per trade, taking every touch versus only the model-selected touches, both measured after costs.
Right: the model's grading skill in three separate time-slices it never saw, versus the same machinery trained on scrambled
labels.



  ONE FINDING CUTS AGAINST OUR OWN BRANDING

  When we decompose the model, the memory and location families do almost all the work; raw order-flow
   features alone barely beat a coin flip (AUC 0.54). Aggression buildsthe level, but it is the level's memory
   that predicts the next touch. We would rather report that than sell a prettier story.

04Where the money actually lives

  This is the section that gives the paper its name. It began as a failure.

  Our first backtests used a tight scalp bracket and looked wonderful. Then we replayed
  the identical signals against real tick-by-tick data and found the result depended entirely
  on which side of the bracket was assumed to fill first inside each bar. Optimistic
  assumption: heavily profitable. Pessimistic: heavily losing. Honest middle: a coin flip. The
  "edge" was an artifact of fill assumptions, so we threw it away and started over at the tick
  level.

  The tick data then explained why, and the reason is the refill itself: around a genuine
  defended level, the median eventual winner first dips 18 ticks past the touch before it
  works. The defence does not happen at the front edge of the zone. It happens inside it,
  where the resting orders reload.

 The refill, one layer deeper: what happens in the order book





  Each bar is resting buy orders at one price. The wall gets hit, price dips inside the zone, and if the defenders are real, fresh
  orders replace the filled ones before the sellers can break through. The trade is to be one of those replacing orders.


 Same signal. Opposite side of the mechanism.

The edge is the refill





Same selected signals, same quarter (Q1), same independent tick engine. The resting-limit entries fill on 64 trades; the
market-order entries fill on 312. The limit fills less often precisely because it demands the flush that defines the setup.

Nothing changes between the two columns except the entry order type: not the signals,
not the period, not the engine, not the costs. Resting inside the zone earns a profit factor
of 1.80; chasing the touch with market orders earns 0.81 and loses money.

This is also why the effect is hard to data-mine into a lie: it is not a chart pattern, it is a
mechanism with a fee attached, and the fee changes sign depending on which side of
the order book you stand on.

06Why this is not overfitting

 A profitable backtest is the cheapest object in finance. Anyone who mines enough
  combinations finds a curve that goes up. So we attacked our own result seven ways. It
  survives all seven. This is the strongest claim in the paper.


  01   The hindsight audit. Every feature was tested for information leaks. One failed: a stored field that
         predicted outcomes even at a zone's firsttouch, impossible without contamination. Removed and rebuilt
          cleanly. The edge stood without it.

  02   The scrambled-label placebo. Retrain the identical pipeline on shuffled labels: grading skill collapses to
        a coin flip, and only 0.2% of shuffles match the real result. The machinery cannot manufacture an edge
        from noise.

  03    Strict chronological split. All choices were made on the first half of the data; only the untouched second
          half is quoted throughout this paper.

  04   The reverse split. Train on the latedata, test on the earlydata. The edge holds, slightly stronger. A one-
        regime artifact fails exactly this test.

  05    Rotating folds. Three time-slices, each held out in turn: mean R +0.14 to +0.22, win rate 63–67%,
         grading skill 0.61–0.64 in every slice. No single lucky period carries the result.

  06   The parameter plateau. All 100 exit configurations: performance on the first half of the data predicts
        the second half with rank correlation 0.92, and the whole neighbourhood of the deployed setting stays
          positive. The next page shows it.

  07   A mechanism, not a pattern. Overfit patterns do not care how you execute; a real liquidity mechanism
        does. The profit lives exactly where the mechanism says it must, on the passive side of the level, and
         dies when you chase.

07Inside a funded-account evaluation

 A per-trade edge of +0.14R is modest. What makes it valuable is its shape: many small
  bounded losses, steady drift, and a fat right tail from the 3-to-1 target. That shape is
  close to ideal for a prop evaluation, which is a race to +$3,000 before a $2,000 trailing
  drawdown, not a race for maximum profit.

 We replayed the held-out daily results 4,000 times through a Topstep-style 50K
  evaluation, with the consistency rule enforced and one self-imposed policy: stop trading
  any day that reaches −4R.


 The evaluation as a barrier problem





  Read the left panel as a fan of possible futures. Almost every path clears the target; only
  the unluckiest sequences of losing days brush the fail line. The right panel is the headline:
  at $80 of risk per R, the strategy clears a Topstep-style evaluation 92% of the time. The
  rest of this chapter takes that apart: how size trades against speed, why the daily stop
  matters so much, and what a pass is actually worth.

Size sets the trade-off between odds and speed

Bet size is the one dial you fully control, and it works in two directions at once. Risk less
per trade and the trailing drawdown almost never catches you, but it takes many more
trading days to grind out the $3,000 target. Risk more and you get there faster, at the cost
of a few percent of pass rate. There is no free lunch, only a curve you get to choose a
point on.

The daily stop, and what a pass is worth

The single most powerful rule we found has nothing to do with the entry: stop trading for
the day once you are down four risk units. A trailing-drawdown evaluation is
asymmetric: one runaway losing day ends the account, while no single good day is worth
the same in reverse. Cutting the tail of bad days off is worth about fourteen points of pass
rate at every size we tested.

The −4R daily stop, measured





Passing is only half the economics. The payout on a funded account is structurally
capped near $2,000 per cycle by the same trailing drawdown, and no edge, however
large, lifts that ceiling. The lever for total income is not size, it is count: run several funded
accounts in parallel and the chance that at least one reaches a payout climbs fast.

Reach a payout: scale by accounts, not by size





A single funded account reaches a first payout ~96% of the time at $80 per R; three in parallel make at least one payout a
near-certainty. Many small, capped, high-probability payouts, not one big score.

08Limitations, stated plainly

  The out-of-sample window is 79 sessions, one regime slice, and the most recent quarter
  lost money. Limit fills assume a fill when price trades at or through the level; real queues
  are less kind. Costs are modelled, not lived.

  The per-trade edge is thin enough that discipline, not the signal, decides whether it
  survives contact with a human. And every number here is historical simulation: it
  describes what was, not what will be. Nothing in this paper is financial advice, and no
  result guarantees any future outcome.
